Research

Our research group operates at the intersection of materials science, chemistry, and environmental engineering, with a particular focus on Flash Joule Heating (FJH) and related extreme-condition synthesis technologies. By combining ultrafast heating, high temperatures, and rapid quenching, we investigate chemical transformations and materials evolution under highly transient conditions that are difficult to access through conventional thermal processing. Our research is centered on three interconnected themes: chemical bond activation and cleavage, elemental migration and redistribution, and nanomaterial formation and structural control. Through these efforts, we aim to establish fundamental principles for controlling matter under extreme conditions and translate them into sustainable strategies for waste valorization, critical-resource recovery, environmental remediation, and advanced materials synthesis.

1. Chemical Bond Activation and Cleavage

We develop electrothermal-driven, rapid, non-equilibrium conversion pathways that transform solid wastes into We investigate the rapid activation and cleavage of chemically stable bonds under transient electrothermal conditions. Extreme heating rates and short reaction times provide access to reaction pathways distinct from conventional thermal processes, enabling persistent molecules and complex waste matrices to be transformed within seconds. Representative systems include fluorinated contaminants (Nat. Commun., 2024, 15, 6117; Nat. Commun., 2023, 14, 6371; 2026, 20, 10498), waste polymers (Nat. Chem. Eng., 2025, 2, 405), composite (Nat. Sustain., 2024, 7, 452), and energy materials (Nat. Commun., 2024, 15, 6250; Energy Environ. Sci., 2025, 18, 6085). These studies seek to establish general relationships among energy input, bond activation, reaction kinetics, and product selectivity under extreme transient conditions.

2. Elemental Migration and Redistribution

We integrate pollutant destruction with resource recovery under the transient electrothermal condition. We investigate how elements released during rapid chemical transformations can be selectively mobilized, captured, separated, and redirected toward desired products. Transient electrothermal environments enable precise manipulation of volatilization, interfacial reactions, phase separation, and chemical trapping over exceptionally short timescales (Nat. Water., 2026, 4, 369; Nat. Chem. Eng., 2026, DOI: 10.1038/s44286-026-00442-8). These principles are applied to the recovery and reutilization of fluorine, lithium, silicon, metals, and other valuable elements, providing new routes that connect pollutant destruction and waste conversion with resource recovery and circular utilization.
